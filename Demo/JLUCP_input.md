## Original Content

Lifelong Learning and Its Correlation with Synthetic Biology Acceptance
Background and Current Challenges
Although synthetic biology is increasingly recognized as an important interdisciplinary technology of the future, educational practices that truly integrate it into public life remain underdeveloped. Current educational activities in synthetic biology:

Often lack targeted design for different groups, relying mainly on uniform lectures or laboratory demonstrations while neglecting cognitive differences and needs across life stages;
Consequently, synthetic biology remains unfamiliar or overly technical in the eyes of most people, making it difficult to connect with daily life and personal well-being.
We have identified that existing educational efforts lack stage-specific strategies. They fail to provide opportunities for individuals—whether during youth, career development, family life, or retirement — to build meaningful and in-depth understanding of synthetic biology.

Our team therefore adopts “lifelong learning” as the core perspective and proposes a challenging guiding question: “Why does synthetic biology hold different meanings for individuals at different life stages? And how can we design suitable learning activities for each stage, so that synthetic biology truly becomes part of everyday life and helps people create happiness?”

We hereby implement a four-step strategy to establish the groundwork for our educational activity design.

Figure 1. Overview of the four-step strategy that underpins our educational activity design, guiding the process from idea to final analysis.

Hypothesis
We hypothesize that there is a positive correlation between lifelong learning opportunities and public acceptance of synthetic biology. Specifically, individuals who have encountered synthetic biology concepts repeatedly at different stages of life (childhood, youth, adulthood, elderly education) are more likely to demonstrate openness, curiosity, and trust compared to those who have never been systematically exposed.

Survey and Data Collection
To rigorously examine the relationship between lifelong learning and public acceptance of synthetic biology, we designed a structured questionnaire that directly measured two core dimensions (For detailed questionnare design, please refer to the appendix at the end of this page):

Degree of Lifelong Learning Exposure – quantified by the number and diversity of educational touchpoints participants had experienced (e.g., children’s storybook sessions, high school design challenges, university lectures, elderly health seminars).

Acceptance of Synthetic Biology – assessed through a Likert-scale evaluation of perceived safety, societal value, and willingness to endorse or participate in synbio-related applications.

Participants were recruited from both experimental groups (those who had engaged with our lifelong learning activities) and control groups (with little to no exposure). This sampling strategy allowed us to capture a wide variance in lifelong learning levels and to test whether cumulative exposure corresponded to shifts in acceptance.

Data Analysis
Figure 2. Linear regression analysis showing a significant positive correlation between lifelong learning exposure and acceptance of synthetic biology.

Using linear regression, we identified a statistically significant positive correlation between lifelong learning exposure and acceptance of synthetic biology. Specifically, participants with greater cumulative exposure scored consistently higher on acceptance metrics. For example:

Children who engaged with the Cell Factory Storybook demonstrated stronger imaginative associations with microbes, which were reinforced in later school activities.

University students with prior high school workshop experience reported greater enthusiasm and perceived societal relevance of synbio applications.

Elderly participants who attended health-oriented seminars exhibited lower skepticism and a clearer link between synbio and personal well-being.

Regression results confirmed that the degree of lifelong learning exposure significantly predicted acceptance scores (β > 0, r ≈ +0.74, p < 0.01). These findings indicate that lifelong learning acts as a cumulative driver: repeated and age-tailored educational encounters build familiarity, reduce misconceptions, and foster more positive attitudes toward synthetic biology.

Conclusion
The continuous design of lifelong learning is not only an educational concept but also a key pathway to fostering positive public recognition of synthetic biology.

First, continuity enables the public to move gradually from curiosity to understanding, and from understanding to application and trust, through repeated exposure and progressive engagement. If educational activities are limited to one-off popular science lectures, the public’s impression of synthetic biology often remains fragmented and superficial. In contrast, when individuals encounter relevant content at different stages of life, this “continuous exposure” reinforces memory, deepens understanding, and allows synthetic biology to be internalized as reliable knowledge and a useful tool in everyday life.

Second, situatedness embeds synthetic biology into contexts that address real concerns at various life stages, so it is no longer perceived as “exclusive knowledge from advanced laboratories.” Children may understand it through stories that “bacteria are little helpers”; young people may realize through projects that “synthetic biology can transform future energy”; adults may see its role in “improving health and industrial competitiveness”; and the elderly may experience its benefits in “slowing aging, improving sleep, and maintaining gut health.” These contextual mappings help the public transform synthetic biology from an abstract “science” into a “necessity” closely linked to personal well-being.

Finally, the sociality of lifelong learning creates a ripple effect that goes beyond individual growth and forms a cycle of social knowledge. Students share what they learn with their families; adults bring synthetic biology concepts into workplaces and social discussions; the elderly further spread ideas through communities and intergenerational education. This chain creates a “from individual to collective” snowball effect. Recognition is no longer an isolated personal experience but expands into a social atmosphere and cultural attitude, thereby strengthening public trust and support for synthetic biology.

In conclusion, lifelong learning provides synthetic biology education with a continuous, contextualized, and socialized pathway. It not only helps the public gradually form recognition but also lays the social foundation for the vision of synthetic biology to “benefit humanity”. When people at every stage of life can experience the close connection between this science and well-being, health, and the future, they are more likely to actively support, engage in, and promote the ongoing development of synthetic biology.

Objectives and Vision
Lifelong learning — a complete educational pathway from initiation to the frontier: We have designed stage-specific learning modules tailored to different phases of life: from stories and hands-on experiments that children enjoy, to thematic lectures and online courses accessible to adults, and finally to science workshops and interactive exhibitions for seniors in community settings. Each activity is both approachable and scientifically rigorous, offering learners opportunities for continuous growth at every stage of life.

Educational equity — making science accessible to everyone: We intentionally address barriers of age, ability, resources, and background. By combining online and offline formats, we ensure that people in different regions and with different capacities can access knowledge. Through visual aids and low-technology teaching methods, we provide inclusive experiences for groups with limited cultural or technical resources.

Across all activities, our team systematically documented design concepts, implementation steps, and feedback from practice, and compiled these as open-access materials for future teams to refine and expand.

Our vision is that through this educational project, synthetic biology can truly enter the lives of all people—regardless of their age, identity, or background—and become a field of knowledge that everyone can engage with and benefit from throughout their lifetime.

# Part 3 Education Activities


## Activity Category R01

### Category Name

Children


### Category Goal

Children are like pluripotent stem cells, full of infinite potential and curiosity. At this stage of life, introducing the fundamentals of biology through playful and engaging activities can open the door to life sciences, spark lasting interest, and lay a valuable foundation for future learning.

Through these educational activities, children not only “learn by playing” but also develop early awareness of life and science with the attention and participation of their parents. We aim to present biology in a fun and interactive way, allowing children and parents to experience its wonders together, thereby planting the seeds of long-term curiosity and motivation for knowledge.

At this stage, the core goal of science education is to stimulate interest and curiosity, helping children experience the joy of science.


### Included Activities

- Health Lecture
- Clay Cell Model Making
- Synthetic Eco-Town — Designing Microbial Helper Communities


### Representative Examples

#### Example 1
Health Lecture
Design Concept
Recent studies show that the incidence of insomnia and sleep disorders among children is unexpectedly high. Chronic sleep deprivation not only affects their learning performance but also poses risks to physical development and mental health. Based on this phenomenon, we collaborated with Changchun First Experimental Primary School to design and deliver a Health Lecture as the first educational activity for this stage. Using simple language and engaging interactions, the lecture helped students understand what “good sleep” means, why they sometimes “cannot fall asleep,” and how daily habits influence sleep quality. We also introduced our team’s ongoing project on biosynthetic melatonin, using lighthearted metaphors to explain melatonin’s role in regulating sleep and to create a direct connection between science and everyday life.

The Change We Bring
At the beginning of the lecture, we used the metaphor of a “biological alarm clock” to explain circadian rhythms, guiding children to understand the relationship between light, daily routines, and melatonin secretion. When we showed a short animation illustrating how “blue light” from electronic devices interferes with melatonin, many children suddenly realized the connection and eagerly shared their own experiences of struggling to sleep after playing on their phones at night. Through interactive Q&A and small games, they not only learned the scientific reasons behind sleep problems but also mastered practical strategies for better rest—such as avoiding screens an hour before bed, maintaining regular schedules, and creating a quiet sleep environment. The most popular moment was the story of the “Melatonin Helpers”: how scientists use synthetic biology to engineer bacteria that produce melatonin, helping astronauts and people on Earth improve their sleep. This story transformed abstract research into a relatable context for children, sparking their curiosity and excitement about scientific applications.

The Change We Embrace
This lecture not only helped children understand “why insomnia happens and how to sleep better,” but also gave them a first glimpse into the hidden mysteries of health within the microscopic world. During the Q&A, many students expressed strong curiosity: “How is melatonin produced inside the body?” “What does a cell really look like?” Their interest extended beyond simply knowing results—they wanted to see and touch science for themselves. This realization showed us that lectures alone are not enough to satisfy their exploratory drive. As a result, we designed the next activity—clay modeling of cells—where children could create tangible cell models with their own hands, turning abstract structures and functions into something touchable and creative. Through making, they could further deepen their understanding of life sciences in an interactive and imaginative way.

#### Example 2

Clay Cell Model Making
Conceptual Design and Practical Action
Cells are the basic units of life, yet their microscopic and abstract nature often makes them difficult for children to grasp intuitively. To address this, we adopted a “visualization + hands-on modeling” approach, allowing children to gradually build their understanding through practice. The core teaching concept of this activity was the analogy of a “cell city”: the nucleus as the “mayor’s office,” the mitochondria as the “power plant,” and the vacuole as the “warehouse.” Such metaphors helped transform abstract structures into concrete images.

Students then used colorful clay to model major cell organelles, including the cell membrane, nucleus, and mitochondria, and labeled each part. Although the task itself was simple, this “see-and-touch” learning method opened a window for them to understand the microscopic world of cells.

The Change We Bring
During the activity, children showed strong engagement and shifted from passive listening to active questioning, asking why organelles looked or functioned a certain way. Their creativity also flourished—some enlarged mitochondria to highlight energy supply, while others decorated vacuoles to show storage functions. We also noted that compared to traditional lessons, the children displayed far greater curiosity and interaction, observing, comparing, and improving each other’s models. This demonstrated the key transformation we hoped for: from simply learning facts to learning how to think.

The Change We Embrace
We noticed that when children created their own cell models, they were not merely reproducing textbook diagrams but also giving cells a sense of “life” through their imagination. This realization inspired us to go one step further: if children could already personalize and reinterpret the “cell city,” why not provide them with a platform to express even bolder ideas?

Thus, we designed the follow-up activity — the Cell Design Creativity Contest — where students were encouraged to innovate beyond the standard cell structure. They could redesign organelles, assign them new functions, and imagine how “future cells” might look and work. By combining scientific knowledge with creativity, this contest aimed to help children deepen their understanding while fostering innovation, collaboration, and the joy of scientific exploration.

#### Example 3
Synthetic Eco-Town — Designing Microbial Helper Communities
Design Concept
Following the My Cell creative contest, we aimed to further guide students from designing single cells to exploring multi-cellular and multi-microbial collaboration, thereby fostering higher-level systems thinking. The Synthetic Eco-Town was designed as an exploratory activity under this concept.

We constructed a sandbox scenario of an “eco-town” facing multiple environmental challenges—such as river pollution, garbage accumulation, and poor air quality—and introduced “microbial helpers”, each representing a designed microorganism with a specific function. Acting as city planners, students worked in groups to analyze the town’s problems and deploy appropriate microbial solutions.

The Change We Bring
This activity marked the first time children considered life science applications from the perspective of system designers, and their performance far exceeded expectations. In group discussions, some teams proposed “air-purifying bacteria” to improve air quality around schools, showing concern for everyday life. Others envisioned “waste-decomposing bacteria” to create a zero-waste ecological cycle. Still others linked their designs to real-world problems, suggesting the deployment of “nitrogen-fixing bacteria” in farmland to reduce fertilizer use and protect the environment.

The children’s solutions not only demonstrated their understanding of microbial functions but also reflected their concern for social and environmental issues. More importantly, they transformed from passive learners into problem-solvers and future designers, showing high levels of engagement and creativity in group collaboration. After the activity, many parents noted that their children continued discussing how to “upgrade” their eco-town on the way home—a clear sign that the activity had successfully turned abstract synthetic biology concepts into vivid and meaningful experiences.

The Change We Embrace
Through the Synthetic Eco-Town, we became even more convinced that children are fully capable of imagining and applying system-level thinking—from the microscopic world of cells to the macroscopic scale of ecosystems. Their seemingly “fantastical” proposals may not be immediately realizable, but they represent valuable sources of inspiration for the future applications of synthetic biology.

At the same time, we also realized that one-off activities are not enough to support long-term exploration. To ensure these educational experiences can be accumulated, sustained, and shared, we began compiling the Handbook of Activity Design for Primary School Students. This handbook systematically documents successful activity designs, implementation details, and educational reflections—not only as a record of our team’s practices but also as a resource to inspire and support other educators.

## Activity Category R02

### Category Name

College Students

### Category Goal

University is a stage of rapid intellectual and personal growth, where students begin to move from learning knowledge toward creating knowledge. At this stage, they not only possess fundamental scientific literacy but also start forming their own reflections on society, ethics, and the future.

We chose to engage university students and invite them to explore the possibilities of synthetic biology and artificial intelligence together. In debates, they could apply logic and rational thinking to confront fundamental questions such as “Should humanity redesign life?”—practicing critical thinking and ethical judgment. In cross-cultural exchanges and roundtable discussions, they could listen to perspectives from different cultures and learn how to collaborate and communicate with a global mindset. In the “Beauty of Cells” AI art exhibition, they could combine creativity and technology, transforming abstract scientific concepts into compelling visual expressions.

Through these activities, we aimed to help university students develop an integrated set of qualities: scientific reasoning, ethical reflection, global perspective, and creative expression. Our vision was to encourage them to become not only learners of knowledge, but also future builders of life sciences and agents of social transformation.

### Included Activities

- “When AI Meets Synthetic Biology” Seminar
- Northeast China iGEM Exchange Conference
- Lecture: Microbes and Gut Health


### Representative Examples

#### Example 1

“When AI Meets Synthetic Biology” Seminar
Design Concept
The central idea of this seminar was to “ignite sparks of interdisciplinary innovation.” Today’s university students are not only receivers of knowledge but also potential drivers of future technological revolutions. Synthetic biology, known as the “third biological revolution,” is converging with artificial intelligence (AI), the “engine of the fourth industrial revolution,” creating unprecedented research paradigms.

This event aimed to build a cognitive bridge for students, showing them how the “read/write” capacity of life sciences, when combined with the computational power of AI, could disruptively accelerate scientific progress—from drug discovery to new materials development. Through vivid case studies, demonstrations of cutting-edge technologies, and team project sharing, we sought to inspire students’ passion for interdisciplinary learning, encourage them to reflect on their potential roles in this transformation, and attract like-minded peers to join our iGEM team.

The Change We Bring
The seminar drew a large number of students from diverse majors, creating a vibrant atmosphere. With a futuristic design featuring DNA and binary elements, alongside brochures outlining the pathway from genetic engineering to AI-assisted design, we presented a systematic overview of the field.

Team members introduced key concepts such as CRISPR and BioBricks, while highlighting the “accelerator” role of AI. An animated demonstration of AlphaFold predicting protein structures allowed students to see how years of experimental work could be reduced to hours. We also showcased AI’s role in metabolic pathway optimization and automated laboratory platforms.

During the interactive Q&A, students raised questions about technical implementation, application scenarios, and potential challenges. Feedback indicated not only their strong interest in scientific frontiers but also their early awareness of the social and ethical implications of merging AI with synthetic biology.

The Change We Embrace
The seminar made us realize that for university students, simple science popularization is no longer sufficient. They seek not just answers but opportunities for deep intellectual debate and confrontation of ideas. Their questions touched on technical boundaries, ecological risks, and ethical standards—revealing a sense of responsibility as future leaders of society.

We recognized the need for a more formal and structured platform where such complex and contested issues could be thoroughly discussed. This insight directly led to our next educational activity: organizing a formal debate competition, where the key questions raised in the seminar became debate topics. This allowed students to critically and logically explore “how humanity should move forward on the path of designing life,” marking an educational shift from knowledge initiation to deep reflection.


#### Example 2

Northeast China iGEM Exchange Conference
Design Concept
Asthe largest iGEM exchange event in Northeast China, the core theme of this conference was “Synthesis and Collaboration.” We recognized that the growth of synthetic biology relies not only on laboratory exploration, but also on open communication and resource sharing among teams. Despite the wide distribution of universities in Northeast China, a high-level centralized exchange platform had long been lacking.

To address this, we led the organization of this conference, bringing together 17 iGEM teams from Jilin University, Northeast Forestry University, Northeast Normal University, Shenyang Pharmaceutical University, Beijing University of Chemical Technology, Lanzhou University, Yan’an University, Zhejiang University of Technology, Ocean University of China, Tsinghua University, University of Toronto, BGI, and others. Teams gathered both online and offline to share research progress, discuss scientific challenges, and exchange experiences in team building. Formats included project presentations, roundtable discussions, poster sessions, and open Q&A, ensuring that every team had both the stage to present and opportunities for in-depth interaction.

The Change We Bring
On the day of the exchange, dozens of participants from various universities gathered, creating a vibrant and energetic atmosphere. Teams showcased their highlights: some focused on environmental governance, others on medical applications, and some introduced interdisciplinary approaches. During the Q&A, discussions ranged from optimizing experimental design to challenges in public outreach, and even to prospects for industrial applications of synthetic biology.

Many students expressed that it was their first time engaging face-to-face with so many like-minded peers in the region, making them feel that “we are not fighting alone, but part of a larger community.” This interaction not only strengthened inter-team connections but also promoted resource and knowledge sharing. For the first time, a true synthetic biology cooperation network was established in Northeast China.

The Change We Embrace
The exchange made us realize the long-term value of regional academic and educational platforms. Feedback showed that participants wanted more than competition—they also sought regular collaboration and mutual inspiration. Looking ahead, we plan to establish this exchange as an annual event and explore links with exchange platforms in other regions of China. We believe such a collaborative model will help more teams overcome limitations of resources and experience, revitalizing synthetic biology not only in Northeast China but nationwide.


#### Example 3

Lecture: Microbes and Gut Health
Design Concept
This lecture was built on the principle of “life-oriented science communication.” We used the metaphor of the gut microbiome as an “internal garden” to help the elderly visualize the relationship between microbes and health. Working with a community senior service center, we organized the event in a local park.

We explained the roles of probiotics, pathogens, and prebiotics in simple terms, using familiar dietary examples. We also introduced common foods like yogurt and natto that contain “good bacteria,” and guided participants through a set of simple exercises promoting intestinal motility. At the end, we distributed flyers summarizing the key knowledge points for easy reference at home.

The Change We Bring
This lecture truly brought science “into daily life.” Many participants said it was their first time hearing microbiology explained in ways so closely tied to their own diets—knowledge they both “understood” and “could use.” During Q&A, the most common theme was linking gut health to sleep quality. Questions like “If my stomach feels bad, is that why I can’t sleep well?” showed that participants were already re-examining personal health issues through a scientific lens, demonstrating strong curiosity and motivation to apply what they learned.

The Change We Embrace
From this lecture, we not only confirmed the effectiveness of life-oriented science communication for the elderly, but also identified a real demand—the link between gut health and sleep. Feedback made us realize that science education should not stop at “what we want to teach,” but also respond to “what they want to know.” This insight directly inspired our next lecture topic: sleep health, continuing with an accessible, practical approach tailored to their real-life needs.

## Activity Category R03

### Category Name

General Public

### Category Goal

In today’s society, science and technology are transforming our lives at unprecedented speed, yet there remains a wide cognitive gap between the general public and the frontier of research. We chose to engage with the public through diverse educational activities—including interactive games, short science videos, street knowledge challenges, online audiobooks, picture book exhibitions, AI art experiences, sleep science challenges, and creative journal cover displays—to make science approachable and relatable.

Our goal was to help everyone understand the core concepts of synthetic biology, its applications, and its links to everyday life in fun and accessible ways. For example, through games like Werewolf or “Lateral Thinking Puzzle,” participants could learn biological system design logic interactively. Short videos and street quizzes allowed fragmented knowledge to be shared in a playful manner. The blue-light sleep challenge helped people personally experience how scientific principles affect health. Meanwhile, cover design exhibitions and AI art activities inspired creativity, showing how science, art, and imagination can come together.

Through these activities, we hoped the public would not only acquire scientific knowledge, but also cultivate scientific thinking, discover science in everyday life, and remain curious and open toward future technological development. This is not only an extension of science education, but also an important step toward embedding science into culture—empowering everyone to become a “science practitioner” in their own life.

### Included Activities

- Aerospace Science Popularization Exhibition
- Street Knowledge Challenge
- iGEM Tutor Model: Opening the Era of Large Models for Public Synthetic Biology


### Representative Examples

#### Example 1

Aerospace Science Popularization Exhibition
Design Concept
In mid-July, we partnered with the Changchun Aerospace Exhibition to deliver a public science lecture at the main venue. Guided by the core concept of “From the Cosmos to Life,” the lecture bridged the grand narrative of aerospace exploration with the microscopic innovations of synthetic biology. Centered on the theme “Life Challenges in Space and Synthetic Biology Solutions,” we discussed typical problems astronauts face—such as sleep disorders, food scarcity, and environmental stress—and demonstrated how our project envisions solutions through melatonin biosynthesis, microbial food production, and life-support system design. To enhance interaction, we included a live Q&A session and showcased selected storybook illustrations and animated videos.

The Change We Bring
The lecture attracted a wide range of attendees, from aerospace enthusiasts to families visiting with children. Many participants shared that it was their first time viewing space exploration through the lens of life sciences, realizing that “going to space” is not only about rockets and satellites but also about sustaining human life. One parent commented during the Q&A: “My kids were thrilled by the rockets, but after hearing the story of the astronaut koala, they now understand why life sciences matter, too.” The event also captured the interest of university students, some of whom approached us to learn more about joining iGEM and synthetic biology research. Thus, the lecture not only broadened public understanding of cutting-edge science but also ignited interdisciplinary curiosity across different age groups.

The Change We Embrace
This collaboration with the aerospace exhibition vividly demonstrated the vast potential of synthetic biology in interdisciplinary science communication. It showed how the field can inspire public imagination about future space life, while highlighting the value of life sciences in national strategic areas such as aerospace. The success of this event has encouraged us to look beyond disciplinary boundaries: future science outreach should not only tell stories within one field but also connect to humanity’s shared challenges. Building on this vision, we aim to expand our activities into more “cross-field dialogues” that transform space fans and science enthusiasts alike into true “designers of the future.”


#### Example 2

Street Knowledge Challenge
Design Concept
Guided by the idea that “science is all around us,” we organized a street knowledge challenge in a popular city shopping district. The questions were designed to be progressive and closely related to everyday life, so that non-specialists could actively think about science while answering them in a relaxed setting. For example, we began with accessible questions like “Are the bacteria in yogurt good or bad?” and gradually introduced frontier topics such as “Have you heard of gene editing?” Participants who answered correctly received team souvenir stickers, creating a lively and engaging atmosphere.

The Change We Bring
The activity attracted participants of all ages. We observed that while most people had some understanding of basic biology, they were unfamiliar yet curious about the concept of “synthetic biology.” In just a few minutes of interaction, we broke down the stereotype of science being “distant and cold,” sparking genuine interest in further exploration. Many participants came back afterward to ask how they could access more related information—clear evidence that we successfully planted the “seeds of science” in their everyday lives.

The Change We Embrace
The street challenge made us realize that the public is eager for continuous learning opportunities, while one-off street interactions can only ignite a momentary spark. To respond to this demand, we decided to extend these fragmented interactions into an accessible knowledge base and subsequently launched our short science video series. The public’s enthusiasm reminded us that the true value of science communication lies not in temporary excitement, but in providing lasting knowledge connections.

#### Example 3

iGEM Tutor Model: Opening the Era of Large Models for Public Synthetic Biology
Design Concept
As the concluding piece of our public education series, the core vision of the iGEM Expert System was “enhancing knowledge accessibility while lowering the barriers to learning.” In our previous games and story-based outreach, many enthusiastic participants and students who developed an interest in synthetic biology expressed a strong desire for more systematic and professional knowledge. Yet existing resources are scattered across years of iGEM wikis, specialized literature, and textbooks—making retrieval costly and learning difficult.

To address this, we leveraged large language model (LLM) technology to build a centralized intelligent Q&A platform. By integrating the accumulated knowledge of the iGEM community, the system allows learners to efficiently and accurately search for information related to synthetic biology, thus “drawing from the community and giving back to the community.”

The Change We Bring
Based on the open-source DeepSeek 7B model, our team invested significant time in collecting and curating data from iGEM Team Wikis, the official Registry of Standard Biological Parts, and specialized literature in synthetic biology and bioinformatics. We then constructed a dedicated database and fine-tuned the model using both self-supervised and supervised QA paradigms.

The final product, a web-based Q&A system, enables users to query in natural language—e.g., “What are the features of the pSB1C3 plasmid?” or “Please explain quorum sensing.” It can even generate summaries of specific iGEM projects. User feedback highlighted several advantages:

Practicality for iGEM participants — Many new university team members reported that the system helped them quickly grasp competition history and technical background, saving significant time in information retrieval.
Support for deeper learning — Some members of the public, whose interest was sparked in earlier outreach events, used the system as an “advanced tool” for further study.
The Change We Embrace
While the Expert System provided strong support for self-motivated learners, it also revealed its limitations: the platform mainly serves those with internet access and the initiative to learn, leaving behind individuals constrained by geography, health, or lack of educational resources.

This realization prompted us to reflect on the broader mission of education: it is not only to create better tools for those already capable of learning, but also to create opportunities for those who lack them. Guided by this insight, we decided to focus our next stage of work on offline educational equity—ensuring that scientific knowledge reaches learners in real-life disadvantaged contexts, thereby laying a stronger foundation for improving scientific literacy for all.

### Representative Examples

#### Example 1

Short Video Science Communication
Design Concept
As one of the most powerful media forms today, short videos naturally combine visualization with fragmented storytelling. We leveraged this format to tightly connect synthetic biology, computer science, and everyday life—turning complex scientific principles into concise and vivid expressions. The core idea was: rapid delivery, clearing misconceptions, and sparking curiosity.

The Change We Bring
We launched official accounts on platforms such as Bilibili, Xiaohongshu, and YouTube, where we regularly released a series of short videos. The content ranged from introductory explanations of basic concepts to discussions of real-world applications and commercialization, covering synthetic biology across four progressive dimensions. Through animations, skits, and fast-paced narration, we transformed abstract knowledge into accessible and engaging stories.

The series accumulated substantial views and interactions. In the comment sections, audiences actively raised questions about synthetic biology and our project. For example: “How exactly did you make E. coli produce melatonin? It sounds like science fiction!” Such feedback demonstrated that short videos successfully spread “bite-sized” knowledge while igniting public curiosity for deeper understanding.

The Change We Embrace
The short video series revealed that the public’s enthusiasm for science stories far exceeded our expectations. Many viewers commented that they not only wanted to learn themselves but also wished for fun, child-friendly science videos to inspire curiosity at home. In response, we decided to upgrade and adapt our previously popular picture book for children into an “Audio Picture Book Theater”—bringing science into children’s daily lives through stories and characters.


#### Example 2

Audio Picture Book Theater
Design Concept
Our team created the “Ken the Koala” picture book series and adapted it into narrated videos, building an “Audio Picture Book Theater” that combines both visual and auditory experiences. The protagonist, astronaut Ken the Koala, faces a series of challenges in space—such as sleep disorders and food shortages. Meanwhile, research teams on Earth apply synthetic biology methods to provide innovative solutions for him. The series has been released on major video platforms, allowing the public to not only see the story but also hear the science behind it in an enjoyable format.

The Change We Bring
This vivid storytelling approach makes science no longer confined to abstract terms or complex laboratory settings, but instead transforms it into concrete and relatable challenges through Ken’s adventures. Many viewers commented that this was their first time truly understanding “how synthetic biology can help solve real-world problems” within the context of an engaging story. In particular, the core story “Ken the Koala’s Starry Lullaby”, where biosynthesized melatonin helps Ken relieve insomnia, resonated strongly with audiences. The series not only ignited scientific curiosity among young people but also helped the wider public experience how closely science is tied to everyday life. As a result, science became approachable, fun, and even a topic of casual conversation.

The Change We Embrace
Ken’s story moved the public from simply listening to science to truly feeling science. Viewers were no longer passive recipients of information; instead, they actively placed themselves in Ken’s shoes: “If I were Ken, how would I face these challenges?” This sense of immersion made synthetic biology concepts easier to understand and remember, while embedding scientific thinking into their daily imagination. Especially in “Ken the Koala’s Starry Lullaby”, many viewers shared their own struggles with insomnia in the comments, creating an unprecedented resonance between scientific storytelling and personal experience.

Looking ahead, we hope to expand this model into more themes and formats. For example, combining picture book stories with interactive experiences so audiences can not only watch the story but also participate in it; or developing multilingual versions to bring this fusion of science and art to broader communities and international audiences. Ken’s adventures are still continuing, and with them, the public’s curiosity and resonance with science will continue to grow.


#### Example 3
Synbio Stickers — Making Science “Cute”
Design Concept
Science communication should not only be deep but also warm and approachable. Considering the popularity of stickers among young people and online communities, we designed a series of adorable stickers featuring astronaut Ken the Koala and synthetic biology elements. These stickers cover various scenarios, such as “Insomniac Ken” and “Sweetly Sleeping Ken.” Through cartoon-style design, we aimed to transform complex scientific concepts into simple, intuitive visual symbols, allowing the public to naturally encounter science in everyday conversations. The sticker pack was released on WeChat and made freely available for download and use.

The Change We Bring
The stickers quickly gained popularity. They not only became a common way for students and audiences to express themselves in chats and on social media but also unexpectedly served as “amplifiers” for our outreach. Many people, when using the stickers, were asked by friends, “What’s this?”—leading to discussions about synthetic biology and our project. In particular, the “Melatonin Sleepy Koala” series was widely used to joke about late nights and sleep troubles, seamlessly bringing “science + everyday life” into casual conversations among young people. Teachers and community organizers also noted that the stickers could be used as icebreakers in classes or events, softening the unfamiliarity of science through a “cute” approach.

The Change We Embrace
The success of the sticker pack showed us that science communication is not limited to lectures, exhibitions, or experiments—it can permeate daily conversations and online culture. When science becomes a social language that can be forwarded, joked about, and shared, its reach and impact go far beyond traditional science popularization. In the future, we plan to expand interactive digital formats based on public feedback, such as stickers + short science videos or AR filter interactive stickers, making science truly “come alive” within the internet ecosystem and evolve into a shared cultural expression for all.

### Category Goal

Our understanding of Lifelong Learning contains two essential dimensions. The first is the vertical continuity of time, ensuring that knowledge accompanies people throughout their entire life journey, from kindergarten to old age. After practicing the first dimension, we realized that the second poses an even deeper challenge and responsibility. Mainstream education frameworks often serve the majority but inadvertently overlook special groups who also long for knowledge. Therefore, we chose to make inclusiveness the next focus of our work, aiming to spread the seeds of science even further. Our efforts extended to teaching support in remote mountain areas, designing interactive experiments for children with autism to spark their curiosity, and collaborating with professional institutions to explore the possibility of translating core concepts into Braille. In this way, the light of knowledge can penetrate barriers and illuminate every mind eager to explore.

#### Example 1

Teaching Support in Remote Areas of China

Design Concept
In remote areas of China, educational resources are scarce, and children’s understanding of cutting-edge science is often limited to textbooks. To fill this gap, we designed a series of teaching support activities. Through vivid storytelling, simple analogies, and interactive Q&A, we introduced synthetic biology—a subject that once seemed distant—into their classrooms. The sessions covered not only basic knowledge, such as the role of microbes in daily life and the principles of gene editing, but also included open-ended questions like “If you were a scientist, how would you solve this problem?” to guide children to think proactively. Our goal was to help them realize that science is not just a set of terms in a book, but a tangible force in everyday life.

The Change We Bring
The response on-site exceeded expectations. Many children showed astonishment and excitement when they first heard that “bacteria can help produce medicine” or “gene editing can improve plant resistance.” During the interactive session, they eagerly raised their hands: “Can I use science in the future to make our village’s soil more fertile?” “Can we invent a medicine to help my grandfather’s arthritis?” These questions reflected their strong desire to connect knowledge with the world around them. Teachers reported that children continued discussing the content after class and even wrote essays expressing their dreams of becoming a scientist or inventor. For the first time, science became vivid, concrete, and attainable in their hearts—not just a distant concept.

The Change We Embrace
This teaching support activity made us realize that even a short session can ignite children’s passion for science. However, we also recognized the limitations of one-off lectures. Children need more systematic and continuous resources to transform this enthusiasm into long-term motivation. Therefore, we plan to combine future teaching support with online science videos and interactive storybooks, allowing children to access knowledge anytime outside the classroom. We also hope to engage more university volunteers to build a stable, long-term teaching network so that more children in remote areas can enjoy the same exposure to frontier science as their peers in cities. We firmly believe that every child should have the opportunity to see science as a real possibility for their future, not as a distant dream.


#### Example 2

Proteins at Your Fingertips

Design Concept
The true value of education lies in equality and inclusiveness. Students with visual impairments often face barriers in science learning due to a lack of accessible resources, making it difficult for them to experience the intuitive and creative aspects of science. In collaboration with the School of Special Education at Changchun University, we designed a hands-on, tactile teaching program. In the first part, we provided 3D-printed protein structure models, enabling students to feel helices, folds, and complex spatial forms with their fingertips. In the second part, we introduced clay modeling, guiding them to create protein structures with their own hands, turning abstract molecular concepts into tangible, perceivable art. Through multisensory interaction and personalized support, we aimed to break the limitations of vision and allow every student to become a scientific explorer.

The Change We Bring
The atmosphere on-site was warm and focused. For many students, it was the first time they had “seen” the three-dimensional structure of a protein through touch. They traced along α-helices and β-sheets with their fingers and described their tactile experiences in their own words. One student remarked: “I didn’t know knowledge could be ‘touched’.” In the clay session, students unleashed their creativity, shaping helices, chains, and even factory-like functional models of proteins. The soft texture of clay allowed repeated modification and refinement, giving them a direct sense of the scientific modeling process. Teachers noted that this approach transformed proteins from abstract textbook terms into concrete and engaging knowledge. After the activity, many students actively asked: “Can we model DNA or organelles next time?”

The Change We Embrace
This activity deepened our understanding that accessible design is not an add-on, but an essential part of science education. When visually impaired students could understand proteins through touch and hands-on practice, they not only gained knowledge but also built confidence in their ability to explore science. The mission of public education is to ensure that everyone can experience the beauty of science on an equal stage. Inspired by this, we plan to develop a general tactile + hands-on teaching toolkit in the future. This will not only serve visually impaired learners but also help more students enhance their understanding of science through multisensory experiences. The true beauty of science education lies in its ability to be touched by everyone and to ignite each individual’s own spark of exploration.

Reflection: Educational Exploration and Continuous Improvement Based on the DBTL Cycle
Figure 1. An illustration of DBTL cycle.
Figure 1. An illustration of DBTL cycle.

Over the past year in our educational journey, we have embraced the DBTL (Design-Build-Test-Learn) cycle as a core guiding principle for systematically designing, implementing, evaluating, and iterating our educational strategies. This cycle, not merely a methodology for scientific research, has become the driving force behind our continuous innovation and optimization in educational practices. Through repeated exploration and reflection, we have accumulated invaluable experience and made significant strides in promoting synthetic biology education.

Theoretical Guidance in the Design Phase
In the initial design phase of our educational projects, we deeply incorporated three core theories: ​Lifelong Learning Theory​,Self-Determination Theory​​ ​and​​ Constructivist Learning Theory​. These theories provided a solid foundation for our curriculum design, activity planning, and evaluation methods, aiming to foster Autonomy, enhance Competence, and build Relatedness among learners. This approach promotes intrinsic motivation and positive learning outcomes. Concurrently, we uphold three crucial characteristics—Continuity, Contextuality, and Sociability—to ensure that our educational content is not only knowledge-coherent and relevant to daily life but also fosters social interaction and collaboration.

Practice and Innovation in the Build Phase
In the build phase, transforming our design concepts into tangible practices, we launched a diverse range of educational programs catering to audiences from kindergarten to university and even seniors. These initiatives included the Picture books and Clay Cell modeling for younger learners to concretize abstract synthetic biology concepts; various lectures and exchange sessions providing opportunities for middle school students, high school students, and university students to directly engage with cutting-edge science; a collaborative project with Changchun University for Special Education, utilizing haptic 3D models and clay sculpting for inclusive learning experiences; community science lectures for senior residents, connecting synthetic biology with practical health and lifestyle examples; and "Transnational Communication" workshops, building platforms for cross-cultural exchange to promote synthetic biology knowledge globally.

Evaluation and Feedback in the Test Phase
To comprehensively assess the effectiveness of our educational activities, we employed multi-dimensional and extensive testing and evaluation methods. Before and after lectures, we surveyed participants (including public audiences of various age groups) on their synthetic biology knowledge. Combining these data with participant feedback, observations, and literature research, we analyzed the phased differences in learning needs and cognitive pathways. This data not only helped us measure the breadth of knowledge dissemination but, more importantly, revealed insights into learning depth, engagement levels, and the overall effectiveness of our educational models.

Profound ​​Insights​​ and Actions in the Learn Phase
Through in-depth analysis of the test results, we gained invaluable insights, which we then translated into concrete learning and improvement actions. We recognize that while significant achievements were made, such as positive participant feedback, increased interest in synthetic biology, and widespread knowledge dissemination in specific groups, we also faced several core challenges. These challenges primarily include a disconnect between early enlightenment and abstract disciplinary learning in adolescence, a gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application, difficulties for adults and seniors in integrating scientific knowledge into daily life, systemic gaps in the accessibility design of existing educational resources, a need for deeper interaction and critical thinking in some activities, and a lack of sustainable mechanisms and replicable tools for certain successful projects.

Future Outlook: Continuous Iteration and Far-Reaching Impact
Embracing the spirit of the DBTL cycle, we commit to continuous iteration and optimization in our future educational endeavors. We envision our efforts increasingly effective, capable of sowing the seeds of synthetic biology more widely and deeply, cultivating citizens with global perspectives, lifelong learning abilities, and a high sense of social responsibility. Our aim is to continually refine our approach to achieve an education that is more inclusive, understanding, and innovatively driven, ultimately shaping a future where everyone benefits from scientific progress.
